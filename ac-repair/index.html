What usually breaks in a Dallas summer

From June through September, Dallas systems run almost without a break, often with the indoor unit sitting in an attic far hotter than the air outside. That load wears out a short list of parts again and again.

Capacitor

The part that gives the fan and compressor their starting kick. It is the single most common summer failure, and one of the cheapest to fix. A humming outdoor unit with a fan that will not spin is the classic sign.

Contactor

The switch that sends power to the outdoor unit. When it sticks or burns out, the system either runs nonstop or will not start at all.

Clogged condensate drain

Dallas humidity puts gallons of water through the drain every day. When algae blocks it, a safety switch shuts the system off, or water shows up in the ceiling.

Frozen coil

Usually a dirty filter or poor airflow, sometimes low refrigerant. Ice on the copper lines means turn the system off and let it thaw before anyone runs it again.

Refrigerant leak

Refrigerant is not used up. If a system is low, it has a leak, and the leak is the repair. Topping it up every summer is paying for the same problem again.

Fan motors and compressor

Motors wear out with age and heat. A failed compressor is the expensive one, and on an older system it is where the repair-or-replace conversation starts.

What to check before you call

  • The thermostat is set to cool and below the room temperature, with fresh batteries if it uses them.
  • The air filter is not clogged. In a Dallas summer this alone causes a surprising number of service calls.
  • The breakers for the indoor unit and the outdoor unit have not tripped, and the disconnect switch beside the outdoor unit is on.
  • The outdoor unit is not choked with grass, leaves or shrubs. It needs about two feet of clear space.

If those are all fine, stop and call. Turn the system off if you see ice on the lines, hear grinding or screeching, smell burning, or see the outdoor fan sitting still while the unit hums. Running it in that state is how a small repair becomes a compressor.

If anyone in the house is elderly, very young or unwell and the house is getting hot, move them somewhere cool now. Confusion, fainting or a very high body temperature means call 911 before anything else.

Repair or replace

Most calls end in a repair. Replacement belongs to a narrower set of cases: a failed compressor on an older system, a leaking coil on a system that also needs other work, or a system well past fifteen years that is costing more every summer. If you are handed a replacement quote, ask why the system cannot be repaired, what the repair would cost, and how the new system was sized. You are also free to ask for a second opinion.

AC repair questions

How much does AC repair cost in Dallas?

It depends entirely on what failed. A capacitor or contactor is one of the least expensive repairs there is, while a compressor or a leaking coil can cost enough to make replacement worth discussing. The licensed company that does the work sets the price after a technician has seen the system. Ask for it in writing before any work starts, and ask whether a service or diagnostic fee is charged and whether it counts toward the repair.

Why is my AC running but not cooling?

The most common causes in Dallas are a failed capacitor that stops the outdoor fan or compressor, a clogged air filter, a frozen indoor coil, or low refrigerant from a leak. Check the filter and the thermostat first. If there is ice on the copper lines or the outdoor fan is not spinning, turn the system off and call; running it that way can damage the compressor.

Can my R-410A system still be repaired?

Yes. A working R-410A system does not have to be replaced because of its refrigerant, and failed parts such as compressors and coils can still be repaired. Our free refrigerant changeover guide explains what changed in 2026.

Should I repair or replace my air conditioner?

A single inexpensive part on a system under about twelve years old is almost always worth repairing. A failed compressor on a system approaching twenty years, or a system that needs refrigerant every summer, is where replacement starts to make sense. Ask the technician to put both numbers in front of you.
