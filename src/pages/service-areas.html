How coverage works

The line has no trucks to dispatch and no office in any city. What it does is send your call to an independent company that works where you live.

When you call {{ site.business.phone_display }}, the automated assistant asks for your city or ZIP code along with what the system is doing. The call is then transferred to an independent, locally owned heating and air conditioning company that serves your part of the metroplex. If that company cannot pick up, it gets your details to call you back.

The cities below have their own pages because many people there search for AC repair. Each page is written about that city: its electric and gas utilities, its permit rules for replacing a system, the age of its homes and the weather events that hit it. If your town is not listed, call anyway.

What changes from city to city

Who delivers your power

Across most of the metroplex, Oncor owns the poles and wires and you buy electricity from a retail provider you choose, so a power outage is reported to Oncor, not to the company on your bill. Some cities are different. Garland Power & Light serves much of Garland, Denton Municipal Electric serves much of Denton, and parts of Collin and Denton counties are on cooperative lines such as CoServ. Your bill tells you which one is yours, and it matters when you are deciding whether the AC broke or the power did.

Permits for a replacement

Replacing a system usually involves the city's building inspection office, and the rules and fees differ. Several cities also require HVAC contractors to register locally on top of the state license. The city pages say what each city's own site confirms; when in doubt, ask the company whether it will pull the permit.

How old the houses are

Older close-in suburbs have many homes from the 1950s to the 1970s, with ductwork and electrical service that newer equipment may outgrow. Fast-growing cities to the north have more homes from the 1990s onward. Age shapes what usually breaks and whether repair or replacement makes sense.
