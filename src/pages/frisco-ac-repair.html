Frisco: a railroad water stop that grew into a city of 200,000

Frisco began in the early 1900s as a watering stop for steam engines on the St. Louis to San Francisco railway line, and in 1904 residents named the town after that railroad. For most of the century it stayed tiny: 1,845 people in 1970, 6,138 in 1990. Then growth took off. The census counted 33,714 residents in 2000, nearly 117,000 in 2010 and more than 200,000 in 2020, and the Census Bureau estimated about 237,000 by mid 2025. Frisco was the fastest growing city in the country for much of the 2000s and again in 2017.

The city spans Collin and Denton counties, and the Census Bureau puts the median owner occupied home value at about $642,000, among the highest in the area. That means large homes, often with one system upstairs and another downstairs, and a lot of houses whose original equipment went in during the same stretch of years.

What Frisco's green building ordinance means for your system

Frisco adopted a residential green building program in 2001. For single family homes with permits filed after June 30, 2007, the amended rules required ENERGY STAR performance, among other things.

Tested homes

Homes under the amended program had to score 83 or lower on the HERS index, a rating of energy use, with each house tested by an accredited inspector. A tighter, better insulated house usually needs less cooling capacity than an older home of the same size.

Sealed ducts

The rules called for duct joints sealed with mastic. When a technician finds the house will not cool, the ducts are less likely to be the first suspect, which points the search toward the equipment, airflow settings or the drain.

A thermostat on every floor

Each story needed at least one programmable thermostat. If one floor is hot and the other is fine, the problem is often limited to the system serving that floor, so tell the line which floor is affected.

Fresh air requirements

The program also required indoor air quality compliance with ASHRAE Standard 62.2, which deals with ventilation. Some homes have a fresh air duct or a ventilation fan tied into the HVAC system that should be checked during service.

Not every Frisco house falls under these rules. Homes permitted before mid 2007 followed the earlier version of the program, and the program's rules may have changed since. Treat this as a reason to ask questions, not as proof of what is in your attic.

Two electric companies and two gas companies in one city

Frisco's resident guide lists CoServ for electricity and Oncor for outage reporting, plus both CoServ and Atmos Energy for gas. CoServ is a member owned cooperative. In Oncor territory, you pick a retail electric provider and Oncor maintains the lines. A news report on an Atmos rate increase that took effect October 1, 2026, said CoServ Gas serves about 72 percent of Frisco's gas customers and Atmos about 28 percent, and that the increase applied only to the Atmos customers.

Why this matters for heating and cooling: the outage number, the gas emergency number and any efficiency incentive depend on which companies serve your address. Oncor's Home Energy Efficiency program, for example, is only for homes that get power through Oncor's lines. CoServ members should ask the co-op what it offers. Write both numbers on the inside of the furnace closet door so they are there when you need them.

When one floor of a Frisco house stops cooling

Many Frisco homes have two separate systems, and when only one floor is warm, a few safe checks help you describe the problem clearly.

  1. Check that floor's thermostat. Make sure it is set to Cool, set below the room temperature, and has working batteries if it uses them.
  2. Find that system's filter. Upstairs systems often have their return grille in a hallway ceiling. A clogged filter can cut airflow enough to freeze the coil.
  3. Look at the right outdoor unit. Note whether its fan is spinning and whether grass clippings or leaves are packed against the coil. Clear debris by hand with the power off at the thermostat.
  4. Check one breaker. If that system's breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off.
  5. Look at the drain. Attic units often have a float switch that shuts the system off when the pan fills. Water in the pan or a dripping secondary drain line over a window is worth mentioning on the call.

Permits and codes in Frisco

Frisco updates its building codes regularly. The city adopted the 2024 International Residential Code and 2024 International Mechanical Code, both with local amendments, effective March 1, 2026, and the 2024 energy code effective June 2, 2026. Mechanical contractors must register with the city's Building Inspections division, and Frisco verifies state licenses with agencies such as TDLR as part of that registration. Building permits are applied for online through the city's Plans and Permits portal. For a replacement, ask the company to show the permit on the written quote.
