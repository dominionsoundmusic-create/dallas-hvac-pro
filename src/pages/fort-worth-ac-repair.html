Fort Worth: a big city with old and new houses side by side

The U.S. Census Bureau counted 918,915 people in Fort Worth in 2020 and estimates 1,028,117 by July 2025, an increase of about 12 percent in five years. The city covers about 347 square miles of land, and the Texas State Historical Association's Texas Almanac lists it in four counties: Tarrant, Denton, Parker and Wise. In its 2021 growth report, the city's planning department ranked Fort Worth second in growth rate among major U.S. cities.

Growth has not replaced the older city, though. Fort Worth's 2023 to 2027 housing plan reports that roughly 51 percent of its housing units were built before 1980. So on one side of town a technician may find a 1950s duct system that has outlived three condensers, and on the other side a 2021 house still on its first system and under a builder's warranty. The right repair starts with knowing which kind of house you have.

Where most Fort Worth calls come from

The city's 2021 growth report counted more single-family homes in its Far North planning sector, about 53,000, than in any other part of Fort Worth, with the Wedgwood and Eastside sectors next at roughly 24,500 each. Those three areas tell you a lot about the calls an HVAC company gets.

  • Far NorthLargely newer subdivisions, many with two-story homes and large attics. Common trouble: a clogged condensate drain overflowing into a ceiling, an upstairs that will not cool, and first-time compressor or coil failures as builder-grade systems age.
  • Wedgwood and the southwestPostwar and 1960s ranch houses. Common trouble: tired ducts, undersized returns and outdoor units that have been replaced several times on the same old line set (the copper pipes between the indoor and outdoor parts).
  • Eastside and the older coreA mix of early 1900s homes, postwar streets and infill. Common trouble: window units turned into central systems, crawl space ducts and electrical panels that were never planned for a modern condenser.

The attic or closet unit and its drain are worth a look before you call. Many Fort Worth homes put the air handler (the indoor part with the blower) in a hot attic, where a blocked drain line or a rusted pan can leak through the ceiling below.

Storms that hit Fort Worth, and what they do to an air conditioner

The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth notes that thunderstorms bringing tornadoes, damaging winds and large hail can happen in any month, though they are most common in spring. Two storms in recent memory show the pattern.

The Mayfest storm, May 5, 1995

According to the National Weather Service in Fort Worth, a storm struck the Mayfest festival along the Trinity River with hail up to softball size, injuring more than 400 people. The storms that evening caused an estimated $2 billion in damage across Tarrant and Dallas counties.

The downtown tornado, March 28, 2000

An F3 tornado moved east along West 7th Street into downtown, breaking thousands of high-rise windows, the Weather Service reported. The same night, hail damaged roofs and cars in Lake Worth and Saginaw, and a person in north Fort Worth was killed by baseball-sized hail.

For an air conditioner, hail is the bigger everyday risk. It flattens the fins on the outdoor coil, which cuts airflow and makes the unit run hot and long. Wind throws branches and fencing into the fan. After a storm, look at the unit from the yard, clear loose debris from around it if it is safe, and have a technician inspect anything bent or rattling before a long run in summer heat.

Before you replace a system in Fort Worth

The city has its own rules for a new system.

  • The mechanical permit. The City of Fort Worth's permit guide says that changing, moving or repairing a central heating and air conditioning system or its duct work requires a mechanical permit. Permits are applied for online through the city's Accela portal, and inspections are part of the process.
  • City registration and a state license. Anyone pulling a mechanical permit must have a contractor registration on file with Fort Worth Development Services, and the city requires a valid Texas air conditioning and refrigeration license for that registration. A homeowner working on the single-family home they own and live in is exempt from registering.
  • A load calculation. A 1950s house that has gained insulation, new windows or a converted garage may need a different size than the old label says. Ask how the size was chosen.
  • The ducts and returns. In older Fort Worth homes, ask whether the ducts were inspected and sealed. A new outdoor unit on a leaking duct system still cools the attic.
  • The refrigerant. New systems use newer refrigerants with matching indoor coils. Ask whether the quote replaces the indoor coil too.

Power out, or AC out?

Fort Worth is in Oncor Electric Delivery's territory, and the Texas retail market lets residents pick the company that bills them. Oncor fixes lines and meters; your retail provider sends the bill; neither one works on air conditioners. Safe checks sort it out.

  • Every light is off. That is an outage. Report it to Oncor at 1-888-313-4747. Many people set the thermostat to Off until power has been back and steady for a while.
  • Lights work, the AC does not. Check the thermostat mode, setting and batteries, then reset a tripped breaker for the system once.
  • The breaker trips again. Leave it off and call. A repeat trip points to a fault for a technician to find.
  • It runs but blows warm. Check the filter and make sure the outdoor unit is clear, then turn the system off if you see ice on the copper lines and call.

Winter matters here too. During the February 2021 storm, Dallas fell to minus 2 degrees on February 16, its coldest reading since 1930, while the Texas grid ordered rolling outages. Fort Worth homes heat with either a gas furnace, with gas from Atmos Energy, or an electric heat pump, and a fall check catches a weak igniter or a heat pump problem before the first hard freeze does.
