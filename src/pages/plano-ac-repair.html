Plano: the suburb that boomed first and then ran out of room

Plano's name comes from the Spanish word for flat, which fits the land. It counted only 3,695 residents in 1960 and 17,872 in 1970. Then it exploded: 72,331 people by 1980, 128,713 by 1990 as big corporate headquarters moved in, and 222,030 by 2000. Growth has slowed sharply since, to 285,494 in 2020 and an estimated 293,000 in 2025, because Plano is surrounded by other cities and has little undeveloped land left.

A city that grew that fast and then stopped has a lot of houses of the same age. Neighborhoods from the 1970s and 1980s in east and central Plano are well past their first system, and many homes from the 1990s building wave in west Plano are on their second. A Plano house by decade: what usually needs a closer look

A general pattern only. Remodels and earlier replacements change everything, so let the technician confirm what is actually in your attic and side yard.

1960s and 1970s

Likely on a third system or later. Original metal or early flexible ducts may leak at the joints, and attic insulation is often thin by today's standards. Ask whether the ducts were tested before sizing anything new.

1980s

The biggest building decade. Many homes moved to a second system in the 2000s, and that system may now be 15 to 25 years old. Ask what refrigerant it uses and whether a repair still makes sense at that age.

1990s and later

Often larger homes with two systems. Original builder equipment has mostly been replaced once. Ask whether the indoor coil was replaced with the outdoor unit last time, since a mismatched pair can cause repeat problems.

Heat records and the 2021 freeze

Plano has seen both extremes. Its record high is 118 degrees, set in 1936, and its record low is 7 below zero, set in 1930. More recently, Winter Storm Uri drove the temperature at DFW Airport to 2 below zero on February 16, 2021, the coldest North Texas reading in 72 years, while ERCOT ordered rotating outages across the state grid. Heat pumps struggle in that kind of cold, and gas furnaces need electricity to run their fans and controls.

The lesson for a Plano home is simple: know how your house heats before the next hard freeze. If your furnace is gas, the gas company named on your bill handles leaks. If that company is Atmos Energy, which is headquartered in Dallas and runs a training center in Plano, its emergency number is 866-322-8667. If you ever smell gas, get everyone outdoors and make the 911 and gas company calls from there. If a carbon monoxide detector goes off, do the same: out of the house, then 911.

Oncor, your retail provider and the AC

Plano is in Oncor's delivery area, so two companies are involved in your power. Oncor owns the lines and meters and restores outages. The retail provider you chose sends the bill. Neither one services air conditioners. When the whole house goes dark, report it to Oncor. If outlets and lights have power while the AC sits silent, the fault is in the system or its breaker, so the call to make is to an HVAC company.

Because Plano homes get power through Oncor's lines, they can qualify for Oncor's Home Energy Efficiency program, which offers incentives for upgrades such as heating and cooling equipment and insulation. Homeowners and, with the owner's permission, renters can take part, and the work goes through approved service providers. If you are replacing a system, ask whether the job qualifies before you sign.

Questions to ask before a Plano replacement

  • Will you pull the city permit? Plano's Building Inspections department issues building permits. Ask that the permit and inspection be listed on the quote.
  • Did you do a load calculation? A Plano home with new windows, more insulation or an enclosed patio may need a different size than its old unit.
  • Are the ducts part of the plan? In a 1970s or 1980s house, sealing or replacing ducts can matter as much as the new equipment.
  • Are the indoor and outdoor parts matched? New refrigerants require matching components, so a new outdoor unit on an old coil is a question worth raising.
  • What is your TDLR license number? Check it with the state before work starts.
