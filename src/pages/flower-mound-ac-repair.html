Flower Mound: named for a hill, built in the 1990s

The town takes its name from a mound about 50 feet high that rises from roughly 12.5 acres near its center and is still covered in native prairie grasses and wildflowers. Residents voted to incorporate in February 1961 to avoid being annexed by Irving. In the late 1960s the town was picked for a federal New Communities Act project meant to house 60,000 to 70,000 people, but that plan failed, and HUD foreclosed in 1976, according to the Handbook of Texas.

The growth came anyway, just later. Flower Mound had 1,685 residents in 1970 and 15,527 in 1990, then added more than 35,000 people in ten years to reach 50,702 in 2000, an average gain of nearly 13 percent a year. The 2020 Census counted 75,956. So the typical Flower Mound house is not old by DFW standards, but a great many of them got their first air conditioners in the same handful of years, and those original systems have long since reached the age where big repairs and replacements come up.

Two-story homes, two systems, and zoning

Flower Mound's homes carry high values; the Census Bureau puts the median value of an owner-occupied home at $560,200, with 2.78 people per household. Bigger two-story houses are often cooled by two separate systems, one for each floor, or by one system split into zones with motorized dampers and a thermostat on each level. That changes how a breakdown looks. One floor can be comfortable while the other heats up, which can mean a failed second system, a stuck zone damper or a control board problem rather than a dying outdoor unit.

Before calling, a few safe checks help narrow it down:

  • Each thermostat. Confirm the problem floor's thermostat is on Cool, set below room temperature, and has working batteries.
  • Each filter. Two systems usually mean two filters, sometimes one in a hallway ceiling upstairs. A clogged one can freeze its coil.
  • Each breaker. Two systems have their own breakers. Reset a tripped one once; if it trips again, stop there.
  • Each drain. Attic units often have a float switch that shuts the system off when the drain backs up. If the pan under the attic unit holds water, mention it when you call.

The town's replacement permit

Flower Mound's Building Inspections division is direct about this. Installing heating and air conditioning units, adding or relocating ductwork and putting in new exhaust fans all require a permit. The town's FAQ draws the line clearly: removing or replacing a unit needs a permit, but repairing or replacing parts does not. Contractors doing the work must be licensed through the State of Texas and registered with the town, and the town publishes a list of registered mechanical contractors so homeowners can check.

  • Get the permit in the quote. The posted fee for an HVAC replacement permit is $150. The contractor must obtain it before starting.
  • Ask for a load calculation. Owners who have finished a bonus room, added a pool bath or replaced windows may need a different size than the original unit.
  • Match indoor and outdoor parts. New equipment uses lower impact refrigerants such as R-454B, which is why the indoor coil is normally swapped to pair with the new condenser.
  • Compare two prices. If one system of two has failed, ask what replacing just that one costs and what replacing both would cost.

Trees, lakes and the outdoor unit

Flower Mound lies in the Eastern Cross Timbers, a belt of sandy land with post oak and blackjack oak. Grapevine Lake and Marshall Creek form much of the town's southern edge, and Grapevine Lake, a Corps of Engineers reservoir on Denton Creek, started filling in 1952. All that tree cover is part of the town's character, but oak leaves, twigs and pollen collect on condenser coils. Keep shrubs trimmed back from the outdoor unit and pull leaves and twigs away from its sides by hand. Leave the coil cleaning itself to the technician at a spring tune-up.
