Richardson: a city built in one fast burst

The Texas State Historical Association puts Richardson's population at 1,289 in 1950 and 16,810 in 1960, as new subdivisions and shopping centers replaced cotton fields. Collins Radio and Texas Instruments brought engineers and their families, and Central Expressway reached the city in 1954. By 1970 the count was 48,582, and by 1980 it was 72,496. Then growth almost stopped: the 1990 census found 74,840 people, barely more than a decade earlier.

That pause matters for air conditioning. A large share of Richardson's single-family houses were built in roughly a twenty-year window, which means many of them have already been through several outdoor units, and some still run on ductwork from the original build. Newer growth came later and looked different: the Telecom Corridor offices of the 1980s, the University of Texas at Dallas campus, and denser housing near CityLine. The 2020 census counted 119,469 residents.

Power, gas and who to call in Richardson

Richardson is inside Oncor's delivery area and the state's retail electric market. That means Oncor owns the lines and the meter and restores power after a storm, while the retail provider you chose handles your plan and bill. Neither one repairs a heat pump or a condenser. Atmos Energy supplies the city's natural gas through its Mid-Tex Division. If the whole house loses power, that is an outage call. If the lights are fine and only the air conditioning has quit, that is an HVAC call.

Before you pick up the phone, a few checks are safe for anyone to do:

  • Thermostat. Confirm it is set to Cool, set below the room temperature, and has fresh batteries if it uses them.
  • One breaker reset. Flip a tripped AC breaker back on a single time. Should it trip again, leave it off and let a technician find out why.
  • Air filter. A clogged filter can freeze the indoor coil. Swap it if it is gray and matted.
  • Outdoor unit. Clear leaves, grass clippings and anything stacked against the sides.
  • Drain and float switch. If the air handler sits in a closet or attic, glance at its overflow pan. Standing water there, or a safety switch that has shut things off, is worth reporting.

Flexible duct can sag, kink or pull loose at a joint over the decades, and the house simply feels like the AC is weak. That is one reason a careful technician looks above the ceiling before suggesting a bigger unit.

Storm damage and a Richardson outdoor unit

On October 20, 2019, an EF3 tornado crossed north Dallas with peak winds the National Weather Service estimated at 140 mph. Its damage survey says the storm weakened and turned north near Richardson Square, then tracked to the Huffhines Park area before it dissipated as it crossed Jupiter Road. The mayor signed a disaster declaration afterward, as hundreds of properties in the city had been damaged.

Storms like that leave outdoor units dented, packed with debris or knocked off their pads, and hail can flatten the thin aluminum fins on a condenser coil. If a storm has passed and the system is running poorly, turn it off at the thermostat and have a technician look at the coil, the refrigerant lines and the electrical connections before running it hard. Photos of the unit before anyone touches it help with an insurance claim.

Replacing a system in Richardson

A replacement involves more paperwork in Richardson than people expect. The city's Building Inspection department handles mechanical repair and replacement permits through its online system, and a trade contractor must hold a current Richardson contractor registration before it can even create an account. The city's published permit reports show jobs logged as complete HVAC system change outs and condenser replacements. So when you compare quotes, check that each one includes the permit.

  • Permit and registration. Ask whether the company is registered with the City of Richardson and who will pull the permit.
  • Load calculation. A 1960s house with new windows, added insulation or a closed-in garage may need a different size than the unit it had.
  • Matched equipment. New systems use newer refrigerants, so the indoor coil usually has to be replaced along with the outdoor unit.
  • The ducts. Ask for the duct condition in writing. New equipment on leaky ducts still cools the attic.
