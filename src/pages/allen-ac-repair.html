Allen: a railroad town that doubled, then doubled again

Allen began as a stop on a railroad line built north through Collin County, and it was named in 1872 for Ebenezer Allen, a state politician and railroad promoter. It stayed small for a long time, with 1,940 residents in 1970. Then U.S. 75, DFW Airport and the spread of Dallas and Plano pushed growth north. Allen counted 8,314 people in 1980, 18,309 in 1990, 43,554 in 2000, 84,246 in 2010 and 104,627 in 2020, with the Census Bureau estimating about 113,000 by mid 2025.

Most of those homes went up in the 1990s and 2000s, and about 69 percent are lived in by their owners, according to the Census Bureau. Repair or replace: how to think it through in an Allen home

There is no single age when a system must go. These are the questions that usually decide it, and the company you are put through to can answer them for your equipment.

Signs a repair can make sense

  • The failed part is a capacitor, contactor, fan motor or drain problem.
  • The system has had few repairs before this one.
  • It cools evenly and keeps up when it is running.
  • It uses R-410A, which is still available for service.

Signs replacement deserves a look

  • The compressor has failed or the indoor coil is leaking.
  • It runs on R-22, which has not been produced or imported in the U.S. since 2020.
  • It has needed several repairs in a few summers.
  • Rooms upstairs never cool, even when the system works.

Whatever the answer, get it in writing: the diagnosis, the repair price and, if offered, the replacement price, all from the same visit.

The city's list of residential work that needs no permit covers only small mechanical items, such as replacing a minor part that does not change the equipment's approval. A full system change is a bigger job, so ask the company to include the permit on its written quote. The city's Building and Permitting division answers permit questions directly.

Storms in Allen and what they mean for the outdoor unit

North Texas spring storms reach Allen too. An EF-1 tornado damaged about 50 homes in Allen in 2008, and an EF-0 touched down in west Allen in 2019. Most storm trouble for an air conditioner is less dramatic: hail that flattens the thin aluminum fins on the outdoor coil, limbs that land on the unit, and power that flickers on and off.

  1. Before the storm. Move patio furniture, grills and toys away from the condenser so wind cannot throw them into it.
  2. During an outage. Set the thermostat to Off until the power is back and steady, so the compressor does not restart during a string of flickers.
  3. After the storm. Walk around the outdoor unit. Look for dents, flattened fins, a damaged fan guard or debris inside the top grille.
  4. If anything looks wrong. Leave the system off and call. Photos of the damage help if you file a home insurance claim.
