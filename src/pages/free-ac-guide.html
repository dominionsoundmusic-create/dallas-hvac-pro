If a technician has told you the R-410A phase out means your air conditioner has to go, slow down before you sign. The move to newer refrigerants such as R-454B is real, but the most repeated part of the story, a hard January 2026 deadline for homeowners, is out of date. The R-410A phase out in four facts

Most confusion clears up once you separate three things the rules treat differently: making new equipment, installing it, and servicing equipment you already own.

  • New equipmentEPA rules cap the global warming potential (GWP) of refrigerant in new home air conditioners and heat pumps at 700. GWP measures how much heat a gas traps compared with carbon dioxide. The book puts R-410A at 2,088, so new systems now use R-454B (466) or R-32 (675).
  • InstallationThe EPA's own sector table now says there is no installation compliance date for systems whose specified components were all made or imported before January 1, 2025. The final rule came in May 2026 and took effect July 27, 2026, so leftover R-410A equipment can still be installed until stock runs out.
  • ServicingRepairing, maintaining and recharging an installed system was never restricted. As the book puts it, a new R-410A compressor or coil can go into the system you own, with no end date.
  • SupplySeparately, the AIM Act has the EPA cutting HFC production and consumption to 15 percent of historic levels by 2036 in steps. Fewer new pounds over time means service prices that rise in jumps, not a ban on repairs.

The book also draws the line where a repair turns into a new installation: replacing the outdoor condenser and the indoor coil together counts as putting in a new system, so the rules for new equipment apply. Replacing one failed part is a repair. When a company says a part cannot be replaced because of the phase out, the book's advice is to ask which part and why.

What is inside the book

The Refrigerant Changeover opens with an epigraph from Proverbs 18:17, "The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him," and that is the method of the whole book: read the primary rule, then compare it with what is being said about it.

The rules (chapters one to four). How the deadline was set, softened and removed, why the wrong version still sits at the top of search results, the stepped phasedown, and the repair versus installation line. The author calls chapter four the one that saves people money.

The money (chapters five, six, ten and nineteen). Why published R-410A prices disagree so widely: some are wholesale, some are billed to the homeowner, and some bundle labor and the leak search into a per pound figure. The book reports those figures as published, not as its own estimates, and gives three questions that make any recharge quote readable. It also covers the end of the 25C credit and how SEER2, EER2 and HSPF2 ratings work.

What to do with your situation

Your system works

The rules ask nothing of you. Keep the filter fresh, book a spring tune-up, and note two things from the label on the outdoor unit: its manufacture date and its refrigerant. Anyone quoting you later will start with those.

It is cooling poorly

Check the thermostat setting and the filter first. If a technician finds the refrigerant low, there is a leak, because refrigerant does not get used up. The book's line for it: a recharge without fixing the leak "is a subscription, not a repair." See AC repair.

You have a repair quote

Get the failed part named and the price in writing. If refrigerant is on the bill, ask how many pounds, whether it is a top-up or a full charge, and whether the per pound price includes labor and the leak check.

You are told to replace because of the rules

Ask the book's key question: "Is this being replaced because the part has failed and repair does not make economic sense, or because of the refrigerant rules?" Only the first is a reason.

Why it matters in Dallas-Fort Worth

North Texas works an air conditioner hard. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth records an average first 100 degree day at DFW of July 1 and an average last one of August 27. The record is 71 days of 100 degrees or more in 2011, and the longest streak is 42 days in a row, in 1980. Long run times are when tired parts quit, and late summer is when replacement decisions get made fast.

That is why the book's planning advice fits this area so well. Its last chapter suggests that if an older system fails in August and the fix is modest, a "bridge" repair can buy time to replace it in spring or fall, with several quotes, a real load calculation and nobody waiting in your living room. The author's test for any such repair: ask whether it will reliably get you through the season.

On incentives, keep expectations local and current. The federal 25C credit is gone for 2026 installations. Oncor, which owns the poles and wires across much of DFW but does not sell electricity, runs a Home Energy Efficiency Program with incentives for HVAC replacements, offered through participating service providers when the program is open. Ask the company quoting you whether it takes part, and check the details on Oncor's site yourself rather than taking a rebate figure on a quote at face value.

Safety note. Leave adding refrigerant and finding leaks to a certified technician. The EPA allows only Section 608 certified technicians to buy refrigerant for home air conditioning, and every recharge should come with a leak search. If you smell gas from a furnace, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone out and call 911.
