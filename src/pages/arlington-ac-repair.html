Arlington: a city that grew more than forty times over in fifty years

The Texas State Historical Association counts 7,692 people in Arlington in 1950. The General Motors assembly plant opened in January 1954, Six Flags Over Texas followed in 1961, and the city reached 89,723 people by 1970, 261,717 by 1990 and 332,969 by 2000. The 2020 Census found 394,266 residents.

Since then growth has slowed. The Census Bureau estimates 402,134 people in July 2025, up only about 2 percent, on roughly 96 square miles of land. In plain terms, Arlington is mostly built out. Since most of its growth came between 1960 and 2000, a large share of its homes are now roughly 25 to 65 years old, which puts many of them in the same stage of life: original ducts, a third or fourth outdoor unit, and owners deciding whether one more repair is worth it.

Repairs that come up often in Arlington homes of this age

None of these is unique to Arlington, but a city with so many houses from the 1970s and 1980s sees them more than most. Knowing the likely culprits helps you follow what the technician tells you.

  • Mismatched indoor and outdoor partsWhen only the outdoor condenser was replaced years ago, the indoor coil may be decades older and a poor match. It can freeze, leak or limit cooling.
  • Leaky or crushed ductsAttic ducts in older homes come apart at the joints or get stepped on. Rooms at the end of a long run get little air while the attic gets cooled.
  • R-22 leaksA system that still uses R-22 can be recharged only from shrinking supplies. A leak on one is usually where the repair or replace talk starts.
  • Electrical wearCapacitors, contactors and fan motors (the parts that start and run the unit) wear out with heat and age. These are jobs for a technician, never for a homeowner.
  • Drain troubleAlgae clogs the condensate line, the float switch shuts the system off, or an old pan leaks into the ceiling.

Safe checks you can make before calling: the thermostat mode, setting and batteries; one reset of a tripped breaker; the air filter; and whether the outdoor unit is clear of leaves and grass clippings. If a breaker trips a second time, leave it off.

Tornadoes that crossed Arlington

Arlington has been hit directly more than once. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth surveyed both of these storms.

March 28, 2000

On the same evening a tornado struck downtown Fort Worth, a second tornado produced F3 damage in Arlington near Bardin Road and Matlock Road, south of I-20, at about 7:07 p.m., then F2 damage northeast of the Grand Prairie airport. The Weather Service reported no deaths or serious injuries in Arlington.

April 3, 2012

An EF2 tornado with winds estimated up to 135 mph began in Kennedale, crossed Highway 287 and I-20, and moved northwest through Arlington neighborhoods, a path of about 4.6 miles in all, before ending near Lake Arlington along Waterview Drive. Eighteen homes were destroyed and 291 damaged.

After any storm, the outdoor unit is worth a look from a safe distance. Bent coil fins, a fan blade that hits its guard, a unit pushed off its pad or loose wiring all call for a technician before you run the system hard. If you will file an insurance claim, photograph the unit before anything is moved.

Arlington's permit rules for a new system

{{ img('dallas-faq-hero.jpg', 'Older couple reviewing papers and a tablet at a kitchen table with a mini-split unit on the wall', 1344, 768) }}

The City of Arlington's permitting guide lists HVAC replacement as work that needs a permit. If the job is mechanical only, the city issues a stand-alone mechanical permit. If replacing the system also means new electrical or plumbing work, the city wants a single remodel permit covering everything instead. All permit applications go through the city's online portal, ArlingtonPermits.com.

The same guide says any mechanical, electrical or plumbing work must be done by a person licensed in that trade. For air conditioning, Texas licenses contractors through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation. Before you sign, it pays to confirm a few things.

  • Who pulls the permit. Ask the company to apply for it and to list it on the written quote.
  • How the size was picked. A 1970s house with new windows and extra insulation may need a smaller unit than the one it has. Ask whether a load calculation was done.
  • Whether the indoor coil is included. New outdoor units use newer refrigerants and need a matching indoor coil.
  • What happens to the ducts. Ask if they were inspected, and get any sealing or replacement priced separately so you can compare quotes.

Electric and gas service in Arlington

Arlington is inside Oncor Electric Delivery's territory, and residents choose their own retail electricity provider in the Texas market. Oncor owns the lines and restores power; your provider bills you. Neither one repairs air conditioners, so when the lights are on and the AC is not, the next call is to an HVAC company.

Natural gas comes from Atmos Energy, which holds the city's gas franchise under Arlington's code of ordinances. Many Arlington homes heat with a gas furnace in the attic or a hall closet, while others use an electric heat pump. If you smell gas, get everyone outside before calling 911 and Atmos. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave and call 911.
